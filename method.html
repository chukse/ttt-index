The TTT Index · Methodology

How the ratings work

Every rating we put out is chasing one question: how much of what happened on the field was actually the guy, and how much was everything around him, the scheme and the schedule and the quarterback throwing him open. Surface stats won't tell you. They add up what happened and hand the whole bill to whoever's name is on the box score. So here's how we try to split that bill honestly, in plain English, including the spots where we'll be the first to tell you the number isn't worth much.

The one question behind all of it

Assigning credit, not counting production

When an offense hangs 30, who earned it? The play-caller, the quarterback, a soft schedule, a great line? The box score shrugs and tells you the team scored 30. That's the problem we're actually trying to solve: pulling those threads apart and handing the credit to whoever it belongs to.

Picture two chefs and two ovens. A dish comes out great, and you want to know: the chef, or the oven? Off one plate you've got no idea. But let each chef cook in both ovens a few dozen times and the pattern sorts itself out, and you can finally see how much was the cook and how much was the equipment. That's the whole trick. It's the same one labor economists use to tell a worker's skill apart from the company that hired him, and we just point it at football: the same quarterback in different schemes, the same coach with different quarterbacks, and we let the overlap do the work.

So these are fairness metrics before they're anything else. Being fair about credit is a different job than guessing next Sunday's box score, and a harder one. We think it's the more honest one too.

QBIR — the quarterback rating

The QB Independent Rating

Most quarterback ratings grade what happened. Passer rating, QBR, the Sunday-night graphic, they all tell you how the offense did with him on the field, which quietly credits the guy for his line, his receivers, and a dome in September. QBIR asks the narrower question: what's the quarterback himself worth, wherever you drop him? We run the season play by play and pull out the opponent, the situation, and the help around him. Whatever's left is as close to the quarterback alone as we can get.

Scheme is the tricky part, because a passer who's only ever run one system is statistically welded to it, and you can't tell the offense from the guy running it if he's never run anything else. So we lean on the quarterbacks who actually changed teams to pry the two apart, then hand a generous system's lift back to the system instead of the passer. Every card shows that tax.

From there the isolated value gets folded in with charting, plain old production, and a multi-year track record into one number from 0 to 100. Nothing hides behind the total; it breaks into seven ranked factors right on the card. And early in the year, when the sample is thin, we lean on the track record and say so rather than pretend four games mean more than they do.

The Coach Index — scheme value

What the system is worth, apart from the players

Scheme value is the same chef-and-oven idea, aimed at the coaches. What's a play-caller's system actually worth once you account for the quarterback he had and the defenses he drew? It's the same model as QBIR, run the other direction, with the coach as the firm and the quarterback as the worker.

The catch, again, is that it only works when the chefs switch ovens. So we measure exactly that for every coach and call it reliability: how much of his record overlaps with other quarterbacks and other situations. Plenty of overlap, and we trust the split. Almost none, and the "scheme" number is really just his quarterback wearing a coach's name tag.

Strong — ranked normally ◦ Moderate — shown, read it as a lean not a verdict NR — can't be pried off the QB, so unranked

This is why defense reads cleaner than offense here, and we won't pretend otherwise. A defensive coordinator draws a new offense every week, plenty of oven-swapping, so his fingerprint comes through. An offensive play-caller runs nearly every snap with the same quarterback, so a lot of offensive systems only half-separate and a few don't separate at all. When a coach's record can't be pried loose from his quarterback, we tag him NR, not rated, and leave him off the ranking rather than invent a number for him.

Creativity is a different animal, and a simpler one. We count how often a team does something unusual, motion and play-action and RPOs and oddball blitzes and weird fronts, and grade it against the league from 0 to 100. It's a description of style, not a verdict on quality. A minimalist who wins on execution scores low, and that's no insult; sometimes boring is the whole point.

What these numbers are not

The fastest way to lose your trust is to oversell, so here's the honest version.

  • QBIR isn't a crystal ball. It's built to hand out credit fairly, not to call next Sunday. On straight prediction it does about as well as any decent public stat and no better. The fairness is the point, not an edge over the number in Vegas.
  • Offensive scheme value is our softest number, full stop. Caller and quarterback are joined at the hip, so for a lot of coaches it drifts close to raw production. We'd rather flag how shaky it is than quietly bury it.
  • NR isn't a hole in the data. We can see the coach's production fine. We just can't honestly peel it off his quarterback, and saying that out loud is the whole idea behind the tag.
  • Creative isn't the same as good. Exotic is a fingerprint, not a grade, and on offense it barely moves with scoring anyway.

None of this makes the numbers less useful. It just means we'd rather you trust the ones we stand behind than all of them equally.
