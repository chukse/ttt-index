The TTT Index · Methodology

How the ratings work

Every number we publish is trying to answer one question honestly: how much of what happened is actually this guy? Not his supporting cast, not his schedule, not his quarterback. Here is how we get there in plain English — and, just as important, where we tell you the number can't be trusted.

The one idea behind all of it

Assigning credit, not counting production

When an offense scores a lot of points, who gets the credit — the play-caller, the quarterback, an easy schedule, a great line? Raw stats can't tell you. They just say the team scored. Our ratings exist to pull those threads apart and hand the credit to the right person.

Picture two chefs and two ovens. A dish comes out great — the chef or the oven? From one dish you can't know. But watch each chef cook in both ovens many times and the math separates out how much is the chef and how much is the oven. That is the whole trick: we watch quarterbacks in different schemes, and coaches with different quarterbacks, and let the overlaps do the separating.

That makes these fairness metrics first. They are built to be fair about credit, which is a different and harder job than predicting next week's box score.

QBIR — the quarterback rating

The QB Independent Rating

Most ratings grade what happened on the field. QBIR estimates what the quarterback himself is worth, regardless of where he plays. A play-by-play model strips out the opponent defense, the game situation and the supporting cast, and what's left over is the quarterback's own signal.

Scheme gets special handling, because a passer who has only ever played in one system is statistically fused with it. We borrow the two-way model labor economists use to separate a worker's skill from the company that employs him — here, quarterback from scheme — anchored by the QBs who actually changed teams. A friendly system's lift is then netted back out, so a generous scheme gets credited to the scheme, not the passer.

That isolated value is fused with charting, classic production and a multi-year track record into one 0–100 number. Nothing hides behind the total — it breaks into seven ranked, sortable factors on every card.

The Coach Index — scheme value

What the system is worth, apart from the players

Scheme value is the chef-and-oven idea aimed at coaches. It asks what a play-caller's or coordinator's system is worth once you adjust for the quarterback he had and the opponents he faced — the same two-way model as QBIR, run the other direction.

The catch is that the trick only works when the chefs actually switch ovens. We measure exactly that for every coach and call it reliability — how much his record overlaps with other quarterbacks and situations. High reliability means we saw him with enough different personnel to trust the split. Low reliability means his record is still welded to one quarterback, so the "scheme" number is really half quarterback.

Strong — ranked normally ◦ Moderate — shown, read as indicative NR — can't separate from the QB, unranked

This is why defense tends to be more trustworthy than offense. Defenses face a new offense every single week — lots of oven-switching — so a coordinator's effect separates cleanly. An offensive play-caller runs nearly every snap with the same quarterback, so many offensive systems are only partly identified, and a few can't be separated at all. When that happens we mark the coach NR (not rated) and refuse to rank him, rather than hand you a confident number we don't believe. We'd rather show less than fake precision.

Creativity is a separate, simpler thing. It just counts how often a team does unusual stuff — motion, play-action, RPOs, exotic blitzes, odd fronts — and scores it 0–100 against the league. It's a description of style, not a grade of quality. A minimalist who wins on execution is a compliment, not a knock.

What these numbers are not

The fastest way to lose trust is to oversell. So, plainly:

  • Not a crystal ball. QBIR is built to assign credit fairly, not to forecast next week. On pure prediction it performs about like any good public stat — its value is the fairness, not an edge over the Vegas line.
  • Offensive scheme value is our softest number. Because a caller and his quarterback are joined at the hip, for many offensive coaches it tracks close to raw production. We flag the reliability instead of hiding it.
  • NR is a feature, not missing data. A coach reads NR because we can see his production — we just can't honestly separate it from his quarterback. Saying so is the point.
  • Creativity ≠ quality. Exotic isn't better. It's a fingerprint of style, and on offense it correlates only mildly with scoring.
